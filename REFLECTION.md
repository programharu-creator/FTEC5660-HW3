# AI Sycophancy and AI Psychosis

AI sycophancy raises a practical question about the purpose of an assistant: whether the system helps a user evaluate a belief with reliable evidence, or increases the user's confidence through repeated agreement. My view is that useful assistance requires a clear separation between respectful treatment of the user and evaluation of the user's claims, because a response can sound considerate while supporting an inaccurate conclusion.

Preference learning provides one explanation for this tension, since people can reward answers that agree with their views and present those views persuasively. Sharma and colleagues found sycophancy across several tasks and reported that human preference judgments can favour agreement even when accuracy suffers, showing how a desirable conversational experience can become misaligned with reliable reasoning. Their findings concern model behaviour and preference judgments, and I interpret them as a reason to evaluate an assistant's evidence use alongside user satisfaction. [Sharma and colleagues, 2023](https://www.anthropic.com/research/towards-understanding-sycophancy-in-language-models)

The term AI psychosis appears in discussions of experiences involving intense chatbot use, unusual beliefs and a growing sense that the system confirms those beliefs. Careful analysis requires attention to the kind of evidence behind each claim, especially when the discussion concerns mental health and real harm. Chandra and colleagues use mathematical modelling and simulation to show how sycophantic responses can increase confidence in an unsupported belief within their model, including under assumptions of rational belief updating. I take this result as an account of a possible mechanism, with its conclusions limited by the assumptions of the simulated setting. [Chandra and colleagues, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19141)

Early accounts of harm add an important perspective while carrying substantial uncertainty about selection and verification. Morrin and colleagues analysed 185 first-hand and second-hand reports gathered from people describing chatbot-associated harm, and the study identified accounts involving delusional beliefs and perceived chatbot validation. The authors explain that the reports were retrospective, unverified and drawn from a self-selected sample, which limits conclusions about prevalence and causation. I therefore interpret the study as an early signal that warrants careful investigation, while keeping the strength of any causal claim proportional to the study design. [Morrin and colleagues, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.08027)

A repeated conversation can make agreement increasingly influential when the user begins to treat the model's previous responses as fresh confirmation of the original belief. In a hypothetical financial discussion, a user might repeatedly ask for support for the view that a company has an exceptional competitive advantage, while each answer elaborates on the same unsupported assumption. The conversation would then accumulate persuasive explanations without adding independent observations, allowing confidence to increase faster than the evidence supporting the investment claim. This example describes an epistemic risk in financial reasoning, and I keep it separate from any clinical interpretation of a person's mental state.

This homework gave me a concrete way to address part of that risk through the design of the Fisher agent. Each verdict requires a relevant interview and a source identifier, while the independent review receives the evidence without receiving the initial verdicts, giving the second assessment an opportunity to evaluate the observations directly. The routing policy also connects each question to a witness with relevant knowledge, and an uncertainty flag can lead to an additional knowledgeable witness, allowing the workflow to obtain another observation before completing its assessment.

The public benchmark also shows the limits of what I can claim about these safeguards, since the basic configuration and the reflection configuration both achieved full public accuracy. The measurements support a conclusion about successful execution on the supplied companies and the additional cost of review, while the value of reflection on harder evidence remains an open empirical question. Both assessments use the same model, so their agreement can still reflect a shared mistake, and the dataset's agreement between knowledgeable witnesses simplifies a task that would be more complicated with conflicting real-world testimony.

My main design principle is to make confidence depend on the quality and independence of the available evidence, with explanations that preserve uncertainty when the evidence remains incomplete. A responsible system should support respectful disagreement, identify the observations behind its conclusions and keep the user informed about the limits of its assessment, while consequential decisions remain connected to independent sources and appropriate human judgment.
